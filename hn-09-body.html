Snowflake renewals have an odd shape. The contract is negotiated by people who rarely open ACCOUNT_USAGE. The usage comes from people who rarely see the contract. Then, a few weeks before the term ends, finance asks data a simple question: how much should we commit to next year? And data finds out it doesn't have a crisp answer.

I sit between finance and data, so that question tends to land with me. These are the six queries I'd run before the call. Most need ACCOUNTADMIN, or a role that's been granted access to the SNOWFLAKE database. The two that read ORGANIZATION_USAGE need organization-level access.

1. Where the money actually goes

Before anything else, split spend by service. Compute is usually the biggest line, but serverless features, storage, and data transfer add up, and they don't respond to the same fixes.

-- Spend by month and service, last 12 full months.
select date_trunc('month', usage_date)  as month,
       rating_type,
       service_type,
       round(sum(usage_in_currency), 0) as spend
from snowflake.organization_usage.usage_in_currency_daily
where usage_date >= date_trunc('month', current_date()) - interval '12 months'
  and usage_date <  date_trunc('month', current_date())
group by 1, 2, 3
order by 1, 4 desc;

Without organization access, ACCOUNT_USAGE.METERING_DAILY_HISTORY gives the same split in credits, grouped by service_type.

2. The run rate a commitment should rest on

with monthly as (
  select date_trunc('month', usage_date) as month,
         sum(credits_billed)             as credits
  from snowflake.account_usage.metering_daily_history
  where usage_date >= date_trunc('month', current_date()) - interval '12 months'
    and usage_date <  date_trunc('month', current_date())
  group by 1
)
select month,
       round(credits)                                                       as credits,
       round(avg(credits) over (order by month rows between 2 preceding and current row) * 12)
                                                                            as annualized_3m
from monthly
order by month;

The annualized three-month column is the number I'd anchor on. A full year of history includes months when the workload was smaller, so the last quarter is a better guide to next year than the last twelve months. Watch the trend too: if credits are growing a few percent a month, a commitment sized to today's run rate runs out early.

Note the column is credits_billed, not credits_used. Cloud services are only billed when they exceed 10% of the day's warehouse usage, and credits_billed applies that adjustment. Plan on what you pay, not on what you use.

3. Compute you pay for while nothing runs

A warehouse bills while it's running, whether or not a query is. Snowflake now attributes warehouse compute to queries, which makes idle time measurable:

-- Idle compute: credits a warehouse burned while no query was running, last 30 days.
select warehouse_name,
       round(sum(credits_used_compute), 1)                as compute_credits,
       round(sum(credits_attributed_compute_queries), 1)  as query_credits,
       round(sum(credits_used_compute)
             - sum(credits_attributed_compute_queries), 1) as idle_credits,
       round(100 * (1 - sum(credits_attributed_compute_queries)
                        / nullif(sum(credits_used_compute), 0)))  as idle_pct
from snowflake.account_usage.warehouse_metering_history
where start_time >= current_timestamp() - interval '30 days'
group by 1
order by idle_credits desc;

Illustrative output:

warehouse_name  compute_credits  query_credits  idle_credits  idle_pct
BI_WH                     727.4          291.0         436.5        60
ETL_WH                    712.7          641.4          71.3        10

The usual culprit is a BI warehouse. Dashboards fire a query every few minutes, each one wakes the warehouse, and the warehouse stays up for its whole auto-suspend window after each query finishes. Check the settings:

show warehouses;

select "name", "size", "auto_suspend", "min_cluster_count", "max_cluster_count"
from table(result_scan(last_query_id()))
order by "auto_suspend" desc nulls first;

An auto_suspend of 0 or null means the warehouse never suspends on its own. For BI and ad hoc warehouses, 60 seconds is a reasonable place to start. Every resume bills at least a minute, so going lower rarely helps, and a warehouse that suspends sooner loses its warm cache sooner, so measure before and after.

4. Who and what is using it

-- Compute by query tag (or user), last 30 days.
select coalesce(nullif(query_tag, ''), user_name)   as source,
       warehouse_name,
       count(*)                                     as queries,
       round(sum(credits_attributed_compute), 1)    as credits
from snowflake.account_usage.query_attribution_history
where start_time >= current_timestamp() - interval '30 days'
group by 1, 2
order by credits desc
limit 15;

Two habits make this query far more useful. Set a query tag in every scheduled job and BI connection (alter session set query_tag = 'dashboard:exec-weekly'), so cost groups by purpose instead of by service account. And look for the same query running over and over: group by query_parameterized_hash and sort by credits. A dashboard that refreshes every 15 minutes for an audience that opens it on Mondays is a very common find.

5. Storage you pay for and never read

-- Tables where Time Travel, Fail-safe, and clone bytes outweigh the live data.
select table_catalog || '.' || table_schema || '.' || table_name as table_full_name,
       is_transient,
       deleted,
       round(active_bytes / power(1024, 3), 1)             as active_gb,
       round(time_travel_bytes / power(1024, 3), 1)        as time_travel_gb,
       round(failsafe_bytes / power(1024, 3), 1)           as failsafe_gb,
       round(retained_for_clone_bytes / power(1024, 3), 1) as clone_gb
from snowflake.account_usage.table_storage_metrics
where time_travel_bytes + failsafe_bytes + retained_for_clone_bytes > active_bytes
order by time_travel_bytes + failsafe_bytes + retained_for_clone_bytes desc
limit 20;

The classic hit is a staging table that's truncated and reloaded every day. Each load leaves the old data behind in Time Travel, and then in Fail-safe for another seven days. Make tables like that transient, which drops Fail-safe, and shorten their DATA_RETENTION_TIME_IN_DAYS. Dropped tables show up here too, still billing until Fail-safe runs out.

6. The contract itself

-- What's left on the contract, how fast it's going, and when it runs out.
with balance as (
  select date,
         capacity_balance + rollover_balance as remaining,
         on_demand_consumption_balance      as on_demand
  from snowflake.organization_usage.remaining_balance_daily
  where date >= current_date() - interval '35 days'
)
select max(date)                                                as as_of,
       max_by(remaining, date)                                  as remaining_now,
       round((min_by(remaining, date) - max_by(remaining, date))
             / nullif(datediff(day, min(date), max(date)), 0), 2) as burn_per_day,
       round(max_by(remaining, date)
             / nullif((min_by(remaining, date) - max_by(remaining, date))
                      / nullif(datediff(day, min(date), max(date)), 0), 0)) as days_left,
       max_by(on_demand, date)                                  as on_demand_now
from balance;

remaining_now and days_left tell you whether you'll run out before the term ends. on_demand_now tells you whether you already have: a non-zero on-demand balance means usage above your capacity is being billed at on-demand prices. This view can lag by up to 72 hours, and it isn't available to customers who buy through a reseller.

If you're on track to finish the term with capacity left over, ask about rollover before you sign. Snowflake's own billing data has a rollover balance source for exactly this case: unused capacity carried into a renewed commitment. Whether you get it, and on what terms, is part of the negotiation.

Bringing it to the call

Order matters. Fix first, then size.

  1. Queries 3, 4, and 5 find waste. Estimate what fixing it saves, because there's no reason to commit to another year of idle warehouses.
  2. Query 2, adjusted for those savings and for planned growth, is the commitment.
  3. Query 6 tells you where you stand. Running out early and finishing with a surplus are very different conversations.

Your account team will bring its own projection. Bring yours, with the queries behind it. A forecast you can rerun in front of someone is hard to argue with.
