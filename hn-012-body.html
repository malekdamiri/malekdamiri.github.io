Every sales leader has a rep who sandbags and a rep with happy ears. The sandbagger commits $80,000 and books $95,000. The optimist commits $120,000 and books $100,000. Everyone in the forecast call knows who's who, and everyone adjusts in their head.

Adjusting in your head doesn't scale, and it's often wrong. People remember the dramatic misses and forget the quiet ones. Demand planners had this problem decades ago with product forecasts, and they solved it with a number called the tracking signal. It's one line of arithmetic, it runs in SQL, and it tells you whether a forecaster's misses lean one way.

Accuracy and bias are different problems

A forecast can be wrong in two ways. It can be noisy: sometimes high, sometimes low, averaging out. Or it can be biased: consistently on one side.

Noise is the cost of selling. Deals slip, budgets move, a champion leaves. Nobody can forecast that away. Bias is different. It's a habit, and habits can be corrected. If a rep's commits run 15% low every month, you can either coach the habit or scale their number by 1.15 and get a better forecast for free.

The trouble is that a single month tells you nothing. A rep who over-called last month might just have lost a deal. You need to look at a run of months and ask whether the errors cancel out or pile up.

The tracking signal

The tracking signal is the sum of the forecast errors divided by the mean absolute error, over the same window.

If the misses cancel out, the sum is close to zero and so is the signal. If every miss is in the same direction, the sum equals the number of months times the average miss, and the signal equals the number of months in the window. With a 12-month window, it runs from -12 to +12. A rep who over-calls every single month scores +12. A rep who under-calls every month scores -12.

Dividing by the mean absolute error is what makes it comparable across reps. A rep with big deals and big misses isn't penalized just for having big numbers.

In SQL

I simulated two years of monthly commits for four reps. Ana calls it straight. Ben commits about 15% under what he books. Cara commits about 15% over. Dev calls it straight for a year, then starts over-calling by 25% in month 13. Every commit also has random noise of about 12%, because real forecasts do.

-- error is positive when the rep called more than they booked
-- tracking signal = sum of errors / mean absolute error, over the last 12 months
select rep, month,
       count(*) over w as months_in_window,
       round(sum(commit - actual) over w / nullif(avg(abs(commit - actual)) over w, 0), 1) as tracking_signal,
       round(sum(commit) over w / sum(actual) over w, 2) as commit_ratio
from calls
window w as (partition by rep order by month rows between 11 preceding and current row)
order by rep, month;

Ben sits at -12: every miss in two years was on the low side. Cara is close to the top. Dev looks fine for the first year, then climbs steadily once the new habit kicks in. Ana wanders, which is what an honest forecaster's signal does. She touched 5.1 at one point by chance.

The commit_ratio column says how big the bias is. At month 24, Ben's commits are 0.85 of what he books and Cara's are 1.16. That's the correction factor, if you decide to use one.

Where do you draw the line?

Textbooks often quote ±4 as the limit. That limit was set for a different setup, and it doesn't transfer. With 12 noisy monthly forecasts, an honest rep crosses 4 all the time. I simulated it:

"""How often does the 12-month tracking signal cross a limit, for honest and biased reps?"""
import random

random.seed(3)


def signal(bias, n=12):
    errs = []
    for _ in range(n):
        actual = random.gauss(1, 0.25)
        errs.append(actual * bias * (1 + random.gauss(0, 0.12)) - actual)
    return sum(errs) / (sum(abs(e) for e in errs) / n)


print("limit  honest  calls 10% high  calls 15% low")
for limit in (4, 6, 8):
    rates = [sum(abs(signal(b)) >= limit for _ in range(20_000)) / 20_000 for b in (1.0, 1.10, 0.85)]
    print(f"  ±{limit}  " + "  ".join(f"{r:>12.0%}" for r in rates))
limit  honest  calls 10% high  calls 15% low
  ±4           39%           94%          100%
  ±6           18%           86%          100%
  ±8            7%           68%           99%

At ±4, you'd flag an honest rep in 39% of windows. At ±8, that drops to 7%, while still catching a 15% sandbagger almost every time and a 10% optimist about two-thirds of the time. For monthly data over a year, ±8 is a sensible starting point. Run the same simulation with your own noise level to set your own limit, because it depends on how lumpy your deals are.

Dev shows the cost of a long window. The habit started in month 13, and the signal didn't cross 8 until month 20. A shorter window reacts faster but cries wolf more often. That's a real trade-off, not a flaw in the method.

What to do with it

Show it in the forecast call, next to the commit. "Cara commits $140,000, tracking signal +11" is a different conversation from "Cara commits $140,000." It's also fairer than reputation, because it's measured, not remembered.

Coach first, correct second. A strong signal is a reason to ask what's going on. Sandbaggers are often protecting themselves from a comp plan or a manager who punishes misses. Optimists often haven't been asked hard questions about their deals. Fix the cause and the bias goes away.

If you correct, correct with the ratio. Multiply a rep's commit by their actual-to-commit ratio over the same window. Do it in the roll-up, openly, and tell the rep. Silent adjustments make reps game the adjustment.

Run it on the leaders too. Front-line managers and the VP forecast every week. Their bias matters more than any one rep's, because it's in the number the board sees.

Why this works

The tracking signal doesn't care why someone is wrong. It just asks whether their mistakes cancel out. That's the right question for a forecast, and it's one most sales teams never measure. Supply chain planners run it on thousands of products every week. Running it on eight reps once a month is easy.
